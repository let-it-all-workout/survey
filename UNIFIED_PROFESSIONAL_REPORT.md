# Effects of the Use of Handset Amongst Students of Legacy University, Okija

## Chapter One: Introduction

### 1.1 Background of Study
The handset has become one of the most influential personal technologies used by university students in Nigeria. Beyond calls and messaging, students now depend on handsets for academic research, communication with classmates, access to online learning materials, social networking, and day-to-day coordination. In many higher institutions, handset use is no longer occasional; it is integrated into student life.

Despite these benefits, concerns have increased about the negative side of intensive handset use. Students may experience lecture distraction, reduced concentration, delayed sleep, and forms of digital dependency. These outcomes can affect academic engagement and general wellbeing. At the same time, students may still report gains from handset use when it is directed toward academic tasks.

At Legacy University, Okija, there is practical need for institution-specific evidence on how students use handsets and what effects they perceive. This study addresses that need using survey data from 327 students. The report presents a structured analysis of usage patterns, perceived outcomes, and student-generated recommendations.

### 1.2 Literature Review
Research on handset use among students commonly centers on academic performance, attention span, sleep quality, social interaction, and behavioral dependency.

First, literature shows mixed academic outcomes. Some studies report positive effects where students use handsets for research, digital learning resources, and academic collaboration (Author, Year). Other studies report weaker outcomes where use is dominated by non-academic activity (Author, Year).

Second, studies on concentration indicate that frequent notifications, multitasking, and social media switching can reduce focused study behavior (Author, Year). However, self-regulation and intentional use have been found to moderate these effects (Author, Year).

Third, sleep and health evidence suggests that prolonged or late-night handset exposure may be associated with poor sleep quality, fatigue, eye strain, headaches, and related discomforts (Author, Year).

Fourth, social effects are debated. While handsets expand communication opportunities, excessive online engagement may reduce face-to-face interaction and increase perceived isolation for some students (Author, Year).

The major gap this study addresses is local institutional evidence for Legacy University, Okija using current student data.

### 1.3 Aim of the Study
To examine the effects of handset use amongst students of Legacy University, Okija.

### 1.4 Objectives of the Study
1. To identify the pattern and intensity of handset use among students.
2. To assess the perceived effect of handset use on academic performance.
3. To examine concentration, sleep, social interaction, and dependency outcomes.
4. To analyze student recommendations on handset-related concerns.

### 1.5 Research Questions
1. What handset-use patterns are most common among students?
2. How do students perceive handset impact on academic performance?
3. What concentration, sleep, social, and dependency effects are reported?
4. What key recommendations do students provide?

## Chapter Two: Methodology

### 2.1 Research Design
This study adopted a descriptive survey design, suitable for collecting and interpreting student responses on behavior, experience, and perception.

### 2.2 Area of the Study
Legacy University, Okija.

### 2.3 Population of the Study
The student population used for this study is:

- **N = 929**

### 2.4 Sample Size Determination
Yamane's formula was used:

\[
n = \frac{N}{1 + N(e^2)}
\]

Where:
- \(N = 929\)
- \(e = 0.05\)

\[
n = \frac{929}{1 + 929(0.05^2)}
= \frac{929}{1 + 929(0.0025)}
= \frac{929}{3.3225}
= 279.61 \approx 280
\]

Required sample size = **280 students**.

### 2.5 Achieved Responses and Statistical Adequacy
- Actual respondents = **327**
- Required respondents = **280**

Adequacy ratio:

\[
\frac{327}{280}\times 100 = 116.79\%
\]

Population coverage:

\[
\frac{327}{929}\times 100 = 35.20\%
\]

Interpretation: The achieved sample exceeded the required sample size, indicating adequate response volume for descriptive analysis.

### 2.6 Sampling/Response Procedure
Responses were obtained through a structured student survey form distributed to students of Legacy University, Okija.

### 2.7 Instrument for Data Collection
A structured questionnaire captured:
- demographics (department, level, gender, matric number),
- hours and purpose of handset use,
- lecture distraction,
- academic performance impact,
- concentration, sleep, social interaction, and dependency,
- open-ended recommendation comments.

### 2.8 Method of Data Analysis
Data were analyzed using frequency counts and percentages, then interpreted in line with research objectives.

## Chapter Three: Findings and Discussion

Total responses analyzed: **327**

### 3.1 Demographic Distribution

#### 3.1.1 Department
ACC, BCH, BKF, CSC, MAC, MCB, NUR, and POS each had 30 respondents (9.17%), while BUS, ECO, and HIR each had 29 respondents (8.87%).  
This indicates broad departmental representation.

#### 3.1.2 Level
- 100 Level: 88 (26.91%)
- 200 Level: 78 (23.85%)
- 300 Level: 72 (22.02%)
- 400 Level: 56 (17.13%)
- 500 Level: 33 (10.09%)

Lower and middle levels contributed the largest share.

#### 3.1.3 Gender
- Male: 170 (51.99%)
- Female: 157 (48.01%)

Gender participation is relatively balanced.

### 3.2 Handset Use Pattern

#### 3.2.1 Hours Per Day
- 4-6 hours: 115 (35.17%)
- 7-9 hours: 82 (25.08%)
- 1-3 hours: 75 (22.94%)
- 10+ hours: 39 (11.93%)
- Less than 1 hour: 16 (4.89%)

Most students use handsets for several hours daily, suggesting high integration into daily routine.

#### 3.2.2 Primary Uses (Multiple Response)
- Academic research: 115 (35.17%)
- Social media: 115 (35.17%)
- Communication (calls/messages): 106 (32.42%)
- News: 87 (26.61%)
- Entertainment: 77 (23.55%)
- Online learning: 76 (23.24%)
- Banking & Shopping: 74 (22.63%)

Students use handsets for both academic and non-academic goals.

### 3.3 Lecture Behavior and Academic Outcomes

#### 3.3.1 Phone Use During Lectures
- Sometimes: 77 (23.55%)
- Rarely: 72 (22.02%)
- Always: 65 (19.88%)
- Never: 60 (18.35%)
- Often: 53 (16.21%)

Lecture-time use remains common among many respondents.

#### 3.3.2 Academic Performance Impact
- No effect: 101 (30.89%)
- Somewhat decreased: 88 (26.91%)
- Somewhat improved: 66 (20.18%)
- Significantly decreased: 49 (14.98%)
- Significantly improved: 23 (7.03%)

Aggregate:
- Improved: 89 (27.22%)
- Decreased: 137 (41.90%)
- No effect: 101 (30.89%)

The dominant trend suggests more students perceived negative than positive academic impact.

### 3.4 Concentration, Sleep, Social, and Dependency Findings

#### 3.4.1 Concentration Impact
Always (21.71%), Never (21.41%), Rarely (20.49%), Sometimes (18.96%), Often (17.43%) show mixed concentration outcomes across respondents.

#### 3.4.2 Sleep Impact
Slight delayed sleep (26.61%), no sleep issue (25.99%), serious next-day effect (24.16%), and often sleeping late (23.24%) indicate substantial sleep-related concerns in the sample.

#### 3.4.3 Social Impact
A little (28.13%), not at all (25.38%), somewhat (24.77%), and a lot (21.71%) suggest social effect is present but varies by student.

#### 3.4.4 Phone Dependency
Cannot imagine a day without phone (29.97%) was the single highest response, followed by totally fine (25.69%), a bit uncomfortable (23.85%), and very uncomfortable (20.49%).

### 3.5 Health and Open-Ended Recommendation Evidence

#### 3.5.1 Health Issues (Multiple Response)
- Eye strain: 123 (37.61%)
- Headaches: 95 (29.05%)
- Poor sleep: 82 (25.08%)
- Neck/back pain: 69 (21.10%)
- None of these: 68 (20.80%)
- Anxiety without phone: 56 (17.13%)

Most-reported health effects were eye strain and headaches.

#### 3.5.2 Recommendation Themes
- Network-related concerns: 181 (55.35%)
- Responsible-use suggestions: 137 (41.90%)
- Power/light concerns: 43 (13.15%)

Students called for better network conditions and stronger phone-use discipline awareness.

## Chapter Four: Conclusion

This study examined handset use among students of Legacy University, Okija using 327 student responses. Findings show that handset use is intensive and routine for most students, with major use categories spanning academic and social purposes. While a portion of students reported positive or neutral academic effects, a larger proportion perceived decreased academic performance. Concentration, sleep, and dependency indicators also showed notable concern levels in a meaningful part of the sample.

The evidence suggests that handset use in this context is beneficial when controlled and academic-focused, but may become counterproductive when excessive or poorly regulated. Student recommendations further point to practical institutional and behavioral actions.

### 4.1 Recommendations
1. Introduce student orientation modules on responsible phone use and digital self-regulation.
2. Encourage phone-management strategies during lectures and private study.
3. Expand student support campaigns on sleep hygiene and screen-time management.
4. Address network reliability and power-related constraints affecting productive academic use.
5. Conduct periodic follow-up surveys to monitor behavioral trends and intervention impact.
